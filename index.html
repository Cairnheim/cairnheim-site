Nothing moves
unless someone carries it

Cairnheim is a colony builder in the lineage of The Settlers. There is no abstract inventory: every plank, every loaf, every iron ingot is a physical object that a settler picks up and walks to its destination, cairn by cairn, along roads you drew yourself.

Three pillars

The flow is the spectacle

Most builders hide logistics behind a number that goes up. Cairnheim puts it on screen: you watch the bottleneck happen, and you fix it with a road.

🚶

Visible logistics

Goods travel hop by hop between cairns, each leg walked by its own carrier. A saturated cairn queues up in front of you — congestion you can see, not a hidden modifier.

⚖️

Decisions under constraint

Workers, tools, food and road capacity pull against each other constantly. There is always an interesting bottleneck — split a route, add a cairn, or reroute the whole district.

🏰

A colony that is yours

Your map is private and persistent. What it becomes is the product of the hours and the planning you put into it — which is exactly what makes it worth owning later.

The core loop

Cairns, roads, carriers

A road links two cairns — the stone markers that play the role of flags in The Settlers. One carrier serves one leg, and only that leg: goods are handed over from cairn to cairn like a bucket chain.

That single rule produces everything else. A long route is slow because it has few carriers. A busy cairn backs up because it holds eight goods at most. Adding a cairn in the middle of a road doesn't just shorten it — it puts another pair of legs on the network.

When several workshops want the same good, the game uses the original Settlers arbitration: the highest-priority claimant is served first, and its priority halves with every unit it already holds. A favoured workshop can't starve its neighbours forever.

The monument

One ruin on your map, and it is the whole endgame

Every colony starts with a collapsed shaft it did not build. Restoring it is the long project the rest of the economy exists to serve — and it is the only thing that mints $CAIRN.

Three stages

Ruin → Shored (30 stone, 30 planks) → Awakened (200 marble, 200 platinum). Each stage raises the shaft's Strike. That is all it does, and it is enough.

Strike is your share

The network mints a fixed amount per day and splits it by Strike. Your share is your Strike over the whole network's — so the pot never grows when players arrive, the slices just get thinner. Emission is bounded by construction, not by attendance. And the divisor has a floor: on an empty network what nobody claims is not handed to the few who are there, it stays in the reserve for the players who come later.

Thrift is the sink

The base shaft burns nothing and never stops. Every pick you set beyond it eats emberwood — charcoal from the burners, never the mountain coal your smelters need: 40 per hundred strikes at first, down to a hard floor of 12. You buy that efficiency with diamonds — and with comfort. Push the rig further and the bill widens: stone and planks past 150 added Strike, iron and copper past 400, wages past 800. A big rig is an industrial estate, not a purchase.

Comfort buildings — the second lever

A miner who lives well works cleaner. Three buildings, one of each per colony, close a share of the gap between your current Thrift and its floor:

  • Chapel — 3 % · 40 planks, 40 stone, 60 coins
  • Bathhouse — 5 % · 60 planks, 60 stone, 40 copper ingots, 80 coins
  • Temple — 6 % · 80 planks, 80 stone, 30 gold ingots, 120 coins

They are the reason the copper and gold chains exist past the toolmaker. All three unlock only once the shaft is Awakened.

The world

Nothing refills itself

Every resource on your map runs out — and every one of them can be brought back, by somebody whose whole job that is. A forester replants what the woodcutter fells. A fish farmer restocks the lake the fisher empties. And an exhausted rock, coal or ore deposit is reopened on the spot by a geologist: 8 coins a mission, five minutes of digging, and the seam comes back full — not patched, full, so you decide when to send one instead of being nagged.

Nothing on your own ground is therefore lost for good, and nothing is free either: every renewal costs a worker, a walk and a wage. The exception is platinum — it exists only on the expedition isles, and no geologist sails. What you bring back from out there, you will not be making at home.

You start with ten colonists. More are recruited with coins — 8 apiece. Coins are also what a large Cairn Mine pays its crew in wages, so a working mint stops being optional the moment your rig gets serious.

Hunger never kills anyone. Running out of food slows production down to half speed and no further: the game punishes you with a bad hour, never with a death spiral you can't escape.

Beyond your own map there are expedition islands — instanced, hostile, and the only place some materials come from. You send a general, you land, you take camps off bandits, and you mine what you came for.

Where this is going

Ownership later — fun first

Cairnheim is designed to end up with real ownership of land and rare goods on Solana. It is also built on one rule that outranks that plan.

The golden rule of the project: the game has to be worth playing on its own merits. The blockchain adds ownership and trade — never fun. If you removed the crypto and the game stopped being enjoyable, we would have failed.

Not a faucet

No token rains down for logging in. What you can sell is what you actually built: a developed map, a surplus of rare goods, a trained specialist. Value comes from time and skill, not from emission.

Not pay-to-win

Paying buys CAIRN, and CAIRN buys picks for your mine — mining rate, and time. It never buys a carrier, a road or a shorter route. Route planning stays the only thing that separates a good colony from a bad one.

Not a pyramid

Studio revenue comes from trading fees and marketplace fees — activity that already happened, not new entrants arriving. Capped emission, real sinks, a ceiling on offline production.

FAQ

Straight answers

Do I need a wallet to play?

Yes. You sign in with a Solana wallet — Phantom — and the game starts once that session is open. Nothing is installed, nothing is charged, and no transaction is signed to play: the wallet is your identity, and your colony follows it from one machine to the next. It also means the same browser can serve several players without them ever seeing each other's map.

Is it free?

The prototype is free. What access to the full, on-chain game will look like is detailed at the token launch, not before.

Is this multiplayer?

Not in the sense of a shared map. Each player builds on their own map, the way The Settlers Online did it. Players meet through trade, chat, mail and expeditions — no real-time synchronised world, which keeps it cheap to run and pleasant to play.

What is Strike?

Your mining power at the Cairn Mine, and the only thing that sets your share of what the network mints. Your share is your Strike divided by the whole network's, yours included — so growing your own Strike dilutes the pot a little for everyone, yourself included. Nothing here scales for free.

What stops someone from cheating the balance?

A headless copy of the game engine runs on our own server and replays what each client reports. Today it runs in shadow mode — it records disagreements and sanctions nobody. It goes live before any real value moves, and it is the reason the market is not open to strangers yet.

How close is it to The Settlers?

The transport model is ported deliberately: cairns stand in for flags, one carrier per road leg, distribution priorities that decay by half per unit in stock. Several timings come straight from the original — a farmer's harvest, for instance, takes 7.8 seconds because that is what it took in Settlers 1.

What does the token actually do?

It is a utility token: premium crafting, rare recipes, lower marketplace fees, light governance. Capped supply, burned by the crafts that use it. It is not distributed for playing and it is not a yield promise.

Can I lose my colony?

No. Starvation slows production to a floor of 50% and never removes colonists — there is no state you can reach that you cannot climb back out of.

Who is building this?

A small independent project. The prototype, the art direction and the economy are being built and balanced in public, one system at a time.
